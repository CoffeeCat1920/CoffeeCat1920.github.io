Why I Switched to Sway and Tiling Window Managers

Published on October 15, 2026 • Category: Linux / Wayland • 4 min read

Like almost everyone starting their journey on desktop Linux, I initially floated between GNOME, KDE Plasma, and XFCE. They were comfortable, familiar, and closely resembled the floating-window paradigms we've inherited from 90s desktop operating systems.

Then one day, I tried a tiling window manager on Arch Linux. Initially, it felt disorienting. Where was the dock? Where was the start menu? Why did I have to press $mod + Enter just to see a terminal?

1. Zero Window Management Overhead

In traditional floating desktop environments, you spend a non-trivial amount of mental energy resizing windows, dragging titlebars around, and minimizing windows just to peek at a terminal behind your browser.

With Sway (a drop-in Wayland replacement for i3), the window manager does the layout work for you. Open two windows? They cleanly divide your screen 50/50. Open a third? It neatly tiles into the split. Once you configure your workspaces, your entire system flows seamlessly:

  • Workspace 1: Web browser & research
  • Workspace 2: Code editor (Neovim)
  • Workspace 3: Terminal multiplexer / background compilers
  • Workspace 4: Chat & references

2. Embracing Wayland with Sway

Sway brings modern Wayland perks without the historic tearing and display synchronization bugs of X11. Smooth fractional scaling, native multi-monitor refresh rates, and lightweight resource usage make it effortless to run on everything from low-power laptops to desktop rigs.

3. The Joy of Ricing

Tiling window managers allow you to create an environment that feels truly your own. Pairing Sway with Gruvbox Dark and a tuned Waybar isn't just about eye candy—it turns using your computer into an enjoyable, cohesive ritual.

"Once you train your hands to stay on the home row, reaching for a mouse feels like taking a detour."

My Neovim Setup: Fast, Lean, and Zero Bloat

Published on September 28, 2026 • Category: Neovim / Lua • 3 min read

A lot of modern IDE setups boot with 500ms+ latency and consume gigabytes of RAM before you've even typed a single character. Switching to pure Neovim configured with Lua was the single greatest productivity boost in my developer tooling.

The secret isn't installing 100 random plugins; it's selecting a curated core that covers 95% of what you need:

  • lazy.nvim for fast, async plugin loading
  • nvim-treesitter for syntax trees and syntax-aware highlighting
  • telescope.nvim for fuzzy searching files and symbols in milliseconds
  • nvim-lspconfig with native LSP and autocompletion
  • gruvbox.nvim for consistent, soothing terminal colors

The result? A cold boot time under 15ms and a distraction-free writing environment that never lags.
